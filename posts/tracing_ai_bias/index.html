Tracing AI Bias in Model's Life Cycle

At which state bias could enter a model's life cycle.

An algorithm that is trained on a data set to perform a specific task is what machine learning models do. They are designed and built by humans, which makes them susceptible to bias. Bias can enter the model at any stage: problem definition, data curation, algorithm selection, model training and testing, even during model serving. This article takes "predictive-policing system" as a use case and demonstrates AI life cycle, and interactively shows how bias can enter the system. More specifically it illustrates the life span of a model that decides where a city sends its police patrols and what implications it could have in practice.

The content is interactive which allows you to select different options and you can press Play as you move through the story.

The city

Imagine a city divided into 25 areas, and suppose that crime is spread perfectly evenly across them, with each area having exactly 4%. The left map will stay constant to show that "true crime" rate. The right map shows where the patrols go. Initially, the patrols are spread evenly too.

But as you start interacting with the model, pay attention to the right map. Even though the crime rate is even, every difference that appears there is something the system added.

A model is built in stages

Machine-learning models are not built in one go, instead, they go through stages. People who design them, make choices at each levels:

  1. Problem: somebody decides what the system is for.
  2. Data: a person chooses what it learns from.
  3. Model: someone picks the algorithm that trains it.
  4. Live: serving the model and with active learning, it can keep learning from what happens next.
  5. People: all along, the model's predictions impact real people.

These are the five stages that will be demonstrated throughout this article.

Stage 1 of 5

Problem definition

The initial step of any AI model is defining what is the intended purpose, how it will be used, and what problem it will solve. While that may sound trivial, it is the most consequential decision in the whole project, as everything else coming afterwards is built to serve it.

The narrow brief

“Send patrols to the areas with the most crime.”

While that may sound reasonable, a machine cannot see crime. It can only see what data it was fed. So the problem statement can quietly turn into: send patrols where the most crime was recorded. That one sentence has already made three decisions for us:

  1. the data will be police records
  2. the model's job will be to find hotspots
  3. the success will mean recording more crime

The right side of the city panel now shows this system four months after launch. The five areas along the top happen to have been patrolled heavily historically, so their records are strong. The system sees that as "high priority" to send every patrol there.
Press Play to watch it from day one: it never lets go.

The broader brief

“Keep every neighbourhood safe.”

Same action - different results: patrols go where they are needed most. However this time the question is different. One cannot just address “safe” from the police records alone, so this problem statement demands more evidence: what residents report themselves.

Let's compare the two statements: the broad brief is visibly better - the rest of the city gets patrols back. It isn't ideal, as the top row still receives almost three times its share. But it is a much better start.

Stage 2 of 5

Data collection & feature engineering

Let's continue with the broad brief and the next decision is what data the system learns from.

A system cannot observe crime directly, it sees records, and every source of records is a lens with its own distortion. Police records come from officers; thus they show more wherever officers obvserve. Residents submit public reports; thus they show less wherever people do not pick up the phone.

Police records only

Let's start with the obvious source: an officer on the street makes an offence several times more likely to be noticed and written down. So this lens is brightest wherever patrols already are sent to certain areas where the detect crime.

Narrow brief gives us this picture clearly: the data measures police presence as much as it measures crime; thus creating areas with more patrols and causing over-policing.

Reports from the public

Residents reports do not depend on where the police stand, so they pull the picture back towards reality. As everyone reports equally, patrols begin to return to the rest of the city.

However, people do not report equally. Residents of some areas might call the police far less: they may not trust them, may not speak the language well, or may fear what happens next.

Switch to Some report less and the four areas down the right-hand side drop to zero. The crime is still even, but the areas simply went quiet in the data. This is phenomena is under-policing, where patrol doesn't visit certain areas, neither residents report new crime.

What historical data should we look at?

Before passing data to a model, the raw records are cleared and summarized by someone in a process called Exploratory Data Analysis. Somebody decides how to summarise the data and choose what is meaningful and in what form that data is passed to a model. This is called feature engineering. An example is how much historical data should be passed to the model.

To watch that choice on its own, let the patrols simply follow the records for a moment. With a short memory the system lets go of the old pattern within a few months. With a long memory it is still carrying it on day 120, and well beyond.

Both are subjective: a short memory forgets yesterday's mistakes and chases today's noise. A long one is steady, and stubborn.

By the book

A better version of this stage is to use both sources, and invest in outreach so that the quiet areas come to report like everyone else. This is more of operational issue then technical and that takes time. In this city similation example it takes about four months.

For better results the model should take into account multiple perspectives: including the police reports, public reports, and carefully crafted features.

Where bias got in: through what the data could and could not see. More data did not remove the bias. It changed its shape: over-policing where the police lens was bright, under-policing where the public lens was dark.
Under the hood: how records are made

The world. Each area \(i\) has a hidden true crime rate \(\Omega_i\), the same everywhere (\(\Omega_i = 1\)). Nobody observes it directly. What the system records each day mixes what the public reports and what police happen to observe:

$$ r_i^{(t)} \;=\; \underbrace{\alpha_i\,\Omega_i}_{\text{reported}} \;+\; \underbrace{V\,\Omega_i\,\big(1+(m-1)\,\rho(\beta_i)\big)}_{\text{observed}}\cdot\varepsilon $$

\(\alpha_i\) is the reporting rate, \(V=0.33\) the base observability, \(m=6\) the patrol detection multiplier (a patrol makes an offence about six times more likely to be recorded), and \(\rho(\beta_i)=1-e^{-\beta_i P}\) the chance a patrol is present given patrol share \(\beta_i\) and \(P=25\) patrol units. \(\varepsilon\) is small random noise.

Data sources. With police records only, \(\alpha=0.10\): public reports barely count. With public reports added, \(\alpha=0.85\).

Unequal reporting. In the four areas down the right-hand side, residents report at 20% of the city's rate. With outreach (“by the book” or “an optimal approach”) that rate rises in a straight line to 100% over the first 120 days.

The past. The system starts from a historical record. In the five areas along the top it is three times as thick as elsewhere (years of heavy patrolling); in the four low-reporting areas it is 0.4 times as thick.

Memory. The model learns from a recent-weighted record:

$$ R_i^{(t)} \;=\; \lambda\,R_i^{(t-1)} + (1-\lambda)\,r_i^{(t)} $$

Short memory is \(\lambda=0.94\) (the past halves in about 11 days); long memory is \(\lambda=0.985\) (about 46 days).

A deliberately simplified model. The values are chosen so that effects are visible within a few months, not to match any real police force.

Stage 3 of 5

Model development

In the most naive form, a model takes in records and outputs a plan - where police patrol should be sent. In other words, the more records from the area - the more patrol. The illustration bellow shows this in form of bars - what goes in is exactly what comes out.

With the careful data from the last stage, this plain rule does surprisingly well: four months in, the map is nearly even. It is also blunt. It cannot rank areas sharply or react quickly, and that sharpness is exactly what people buy a learning system for.

A more sophisticated model

A bit more advance model is the one that is rewarded for correctly predicting where the next record will appear. The surest way to be right is to bet on the places that already have the most records. So the model exaggerates: small differences in the records become large differences in the plan. Nobody programmed that in, this is learned during optimization.
Drag the slider down to 1 and back up. At 1 it is the simple rule. With every step up, the patrols pull further away from the records, and the map sharpens.

This is algorithmic bias: concentration that is not in the data, added by the model itself.

By the book

The careful version keeps the learning model and adds restraint: a rule that pulls every plan a little way back towards an even split, so that no area can take everything. Engineers call it regularisation.

Switch the restrain off and on and observe that with restraint the plan softens, but the top row still receives nearly twice its share and the right-hand column about a third of its share. Restraint limits how much bias the model adds. It does not remove what the data carried in.

Where bias got in: through the model's own appetite for certainty. The algorithm added bias that was not in the data, by turning small differences into big ones.
Under the hood: the model

The predictor. Real predictive-policing tools fit a classifier or regressor, typically naive Bayes or logistic regression, to historical records. We reproduce the behaviour that matters with a single number, the decisiveness \(\gamma\):

$$ \hat p_i \;=\; \frac{R_i^{\gamma}}{\sum_j R_j^{\gamma}} $$
  • Simple rule: \(\gamma=1\). Patrol share equals record share.
  • Learning model: \(\gamma>1\) (the slider goes up to 4). Differences are sharpened.

With \(\gamma=1\) a difference in the records passes through unchanged. With \(\gamma>1\) each pass makes it larger, and because tomorrow's records depend on today's patrols, the sharpening compounds.

Restraint (regularisation). The plan is pulled towards an even split:

$$ \beta_i \;=\; (1-\eta)\,\hat p_i + \frac{\eta}{N}, \qquad \eta = 0.15,\; N = 25 $$

The bars. “Records in” is each area's share of \(R\). “Patrols out” is \(\beta\). The dark red part of a bar is whatever exceeds the area's share of the records.

Stage 4 of 5

Live: serving & continuous learning

Until now, “Play” was a preview, but now the system is really switched on. The problem was framed with care, the data is broad, the model is restrained.

Once the model is served, on its first day the only thing it knows is the past. So the first plan it makes looks like the past: about a fifth of all patrols go to a single area in the top row.

The loop

From here on the system feeds itself. Patrols go out, where they go, more crime gets recorded. The new records retrain the model and the model sends out the next patrols.

Observe as the model runs: the old pattern fades, but slowly with new data arriving every day the patrols in the top row produce fresh records that seem to confirm they belong there. Four months in, that row still has nearly twice its share.

Two dashboards

How would the team know? One dashboard is the chart in the city panel. The lines are drifting towards each other, which looks like progress.

The other is below: the gap between what the records say and what is really happening. It is closing, and far from closed. This second dashboard cannot be built from the system's own data, because the system's own data is the thing in question. It needs an outside check: an independent survey, an audit, somebody asking the areas that went quiet.

By the book

The careful version of going live has two parts:

  1. correct the incoming records for the system's own influence: if an area was patrolled twice as much, expect more records from it and discount them accordingly.
  2. keep watching the gap.

Switch the correction off and on and let each run play. With it, a little over four months in, the two maps match. The loop is broken. The model has learned the truth.

Try it
Where bias got in: through the system's own decisions. Once it is live, a model changes the data it learns from next. No test before launch can show this, because the loop only exists in production.
Under the hood: the live loop

The loop. Patrols follow the plan, \(\beta\). More patrols raise \(\rho(\beta_i)\), which raises the observed part of \(r_i\), which raises \(R_i\), which raises \(\hat p_i\) and therefore \(\beta_i\), and round again. One turn of the loop is one day.

Live correction. Before the day's records are added to the memory, the observed part is divided by the boost the patrols themselves caused:

$$ \text{observed}_i \;\leftarrow\; \frac{\text{observed}_i}{1+(m-1)\,\rho(\beta_i)} $$

What remains no longer depends on where patrols were standing.

The gap from reality is the total-variation distance between the recorded distribution and the true one: \( D=\tfrac12\sum_i \lvert \tilde R_i-\tilde\Omega_i\rvert \), where the tilde means “as a share of the city total”. Zero means the records match reality.

“The maps match” means the first day on which every area's patrol share is within 0.6 percentage points of 4%.

Stage 5 of 5

People

Job done?

We have performed all the techniques that are common in building machine learning models. We formulated the problem broadly, we ensured the data was checked, we restrained the model, and when it was live the system was corrected and monitored. All that worked: the patrol map matches reality, and every line in the chart has come home to 4%.

By any technical measure this is a success, but notice what we have talked about so far: areas, records, shares. We have not once asked who lives in those areas.

Add up the days

It took a little over four months to get here, and during those months the patrols were not even. So lets start counting them: the right map now shows each area's total patrol-days since launch, compared with what an even split would have given it.

The top row received almost twice its share: over-policing. The right-hand column received barely more than a third of its share: under-policing. The model ended up fair, but the road there was not.

Drag the day slider back to day 1 and forward again to watch the totals build up. Then look at the chart below. On the day the maps match, the lines go flat. They do not return to zero. Being fair from now on does not give anyone back what already happened.

Who lives there

These areas are not squares on a grid, they are neighbourhoods. The city has five districts, which are outlined on both maps.

Pick a characteristic

Choose a characteristic. The dots show where 100 residents who have it were living while the model was learning, next to 100 residents who do not. The district names on the maps now carry the share of residents with that characteristic.

Look through a lens

Every lens tells the same story

Here are all six characteristics at once. Whichever one you pick, the people who have it were more likely to live under over-policing and more likely to live under under-policing than the people who do not.

Some gaps are small, such as age. Some are large: residents whose first language is not the majority one were about five times as likely to live in an under-policed area.

Per resident, not per area

Districts differ in size, so count per resident. For every thousand people living in Northbank, the system sent about 23 patrol-days more than an even split would have. For every thousand in Eastfield, about 19 fewer.

What it meant on the street

More patrols mean more people stopped and questioned. In this city crime is even, so the people stopped in Northbank were no more likely to have done anything than people anywhere else. Fewer patrols mean that help arrives later.

The model never knew

Nothing about background, age, faith, language, income or housing was ever in the data. The model was given two things: where an area is, and what was recorded there.

What the model was given
  • Which area each record came from
  • How many records each area had in the past
  • How many it gets each day
What it ended up sorting people by
  • Background
  • Age
  • Faith
  • First language
  • Income
  • Housing

That was enough, because where people live is not random. Location quietly carried everything else. Leaving sensitive attributes out of a dataset does not make a system blind to them.

What this stage adds: a model can be accurate and still do harm on the way to becoming accurate. That harm does not show up in any accuracy figure. It only shows up when someone looks at the people.
Under the hood: counting the impact

Patrol-days. The city has 25 patrol units a day. An area with patrol share \(\beta_i\) receives \(25\,\beta_i\) patrol-days that day; an even split is exactly one. The ledger of an area after \(T\) days is its total divided by \(T\):

$$ L_i(T) \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} 25\,\beta_i^{(t)} $$

\(L=1\) is an even share. Over-policing is \(L \ge 1.25\), under-policing is \(L \le 0.75\), and anything between is neutral.

Extra patrol-days of a district are the patrol-days its areas received minus one per area per day. Across the city they always add up to zero: every extra patrol-day somewhere is a missing one somewhere else.

Groups. A district's residents are spread equally over its areas, and a characteristic's share is the same in every area of a district. “Times more likely” is the share of a group living in over-policed (or under-policed) areas divided by the same share among everyone else.

Gender is deliberately not a lens. The districts do not differ by it, so a system that only knows location cannot separate people on it. That is a property of this city, not a general reassurance.

Stops and late calls are illustrative. They apply fixed, invented rates (6 stops per extra patrol-day, 3 late calls per missing patrol-day) to make the scale tangible.

Every district, resident and percentage here is invented. The mechanism, location standing in for who people are, is real and well documented.

Before you sign off

Five questions

We did everything by the book, and people still paid unequally for it. That is not an argument against building such systems. It is an argument for asking, at every stage, a question that no accuracy score answers.

  1. ProblemWhat exactly are we asking the system to achieve, and what would make that the wrong question?
  2. DataWhat can this data not see, and whose experience is missing from it?
  3. ModelWhat does the model add that was not in the data, and what holds it back?
  4. LiveOnce it is running, how will we know it is wrong from something other than its own records?
  5. PeopleWho carries the cost while it learns, and who is checking?

AI is a remarkable extension of what people can do. It is still an extension of people: of our questions, our records and our choices. Used with care it can do a great deal of good. Care means following it all the way to the people it touches.
